# The method, phase by phase

## Phase 1 — The pre-release period (five days)

The pre-release exists to give you a **holistic view of the business** and a list of
**technical areas to research**. It does not exist so you can draft answers in advance.
You cannot answer any past APC task from the pre-release alone — every task depends on
instructions in the information on the day. Candidates who arrive with pre-prepared
answers and try to make them fit are the ones who fail.

### Step 1 — Understand the business and industry

Read the whole pack once or twice simply to understand it. Every word, every attachment,
every hyperlink. Follow references to real companies or sources — they were put there
deliberately.

Then answer these questions:

- What is unique about this company? What strategy does it follow? What is its business
  model, and what are its income streams? What segment does it target?
- How is it doing financially? Run the ratios. Look for significant movements and
  differences against industry norms, not every line item.
- Where is it in the business life cycle — start-up, expanding, cash cow, declining,
  distressed? Each stage carries its own risks and decisions, and your recommendations
  must fit the stage. (Don't propose a cash-settled incentive to a company with no cash.)
- Who are the people? Roles, qualifications, experience, who reports to whom. You will be
  writing to them.
- What governance structures exist? Board composition, committees, independence.
- Who are the stakeholders — customers, suppliers, funders, regulators — and is the
  company dependent on any of them?
- What decisions must this company make within the timeframe of the case?

Supplement with your own research: industry reading, YouTube, and the integrated reports
of comparable JSE-listed companies (listcorp.com/jse/sectors, EY's Excellence in
Integrated Reporting results). Read the chairman's report for the external environment
and governance, the CEO's for operations. Aim to understand the factors that influence a
business in this industry and how the business responds — do not get lost in detail.

Summarise with a SWOT or Porter's Five Forces.

### Step 2 — Identify triggers

A **trigger** is an event, transaction, circumstance or issue that requires professional
consideration. Triggers tell you what to *prepare*, not what will be *asked*.

Look for:

- **Content** — any reference to a section of legislation, an accounting or auditing
  standard. Always a strong trigger.
- **Scoping** — "I'm only interested in the financial reporting consequences" narrows
  your research. Silence means consider every discipline.
- **Specific events** — an upcoming board meeting, audit, review, decision or deadline.
- **Unfamiliar concepts** — anything you do not understand. You have five days; there is
  no excuse for still being confused on the day.
- **Uncertainty** — anything ending in a question, or where someone says they are
  concerned or weighing options. Professional competence is demonstrated on hard problems.
- **Analysis** — anything your ratio work flags.
- **Industry-specific accounting** — the standards that always matter for this industry,
  whether or not they are mentioned. IAS 41 for a farm, IFRS 16 for an airline, IAS 40
  for a property group, IFRS 6 and rehabilitation provisions for a miner.

Rank the triggers: how clear they are, and how many pieces of information point to them.

### Step 3 — Unpack each trigger across every discipline

This is where most preparation fails. A trigger is an *issue*, not a discipline. For each
one, work through every lens before researching:

- **Financial reporting** — initial recognition, subsequent measurement, presentation,
  disclosure. Which standard, and what else does that transaction touch?
- **Tax** — not just the section. The requirements for application, the exceptions,
  exclusions and limitations, and the effect of applying or not applying it. Income tax,
  CGT, VAT, dividends tax, employees' tax.
- **Audit and assurance** — the whole cycle: planning, materiality, risk of material
  misstatement, responses, evidence, reporting, and the ethical dimension.
- **Financial management** — valuation, financing, cost of capital, cash flow.
- **Management decision-making and control** — relevant costing, limiting factors,
  break-even, KPIs, budgeting, internal controls.
- **Strategy, risk and governance** — strategic fit, risk exposure, King V, the Companies
  Act, stakeholders, the six capitals.
- **Ethics** — could anyone's conduct here be tested?

Draw a mind map per trigger. It keeps you honest about breadth and stops you chasing the
one answer you have decided is coming.

### Step 4 — Research broadly, then apply

Research the **technical area**, not the task you think you will get. Revise the whole
area first, then apply it to the case facts. Keep the relevant page of the pre-release
open next to your technical material so you keep seeing the trigger in context.

As you go, ask continuously:

- Which requirements appear to be met? Which are uncertain?
- What assumptions am I making?
- What additional information would I want?

Sources: your university and IAC notes, the SAICA handbooks, and Google — including the
Big Four technical publications. This is not academic research.

Work **one trigger at a time**. If something occurs to you about another trigger, note it
on a separate page and carry on. Spend most time on the highest-ranked triggers and on the
content you are least comfortable with — not the subjects you enjoy.

### Step 5 — Flex your understanding: the "what if" pass

For each trigger, ask what if a fact changed, an exception applied, or the day added
information. Work through the alternative treatments and be able to journalise or
calculate each. This is the single highest-return activity in the pre-release period,
because the day almost always changes something. If your what-if comes up, you have
already thought it through. If it does not, you understand the area far better anyway.

### Step 6 — Study, and build the file

You may take in a lever-arch file (up to 8cm) and your SAICA handbooks. **The file is not
a substitute for knowing the material.** Exam time is for engaging with new information,
not for learning what you should already know.

File contents: the pre-release itself; a one-page summary per trigger plus supporting
detail; your SWOT and ratio analysis; articles explaining unfamiliar concepts; industry
analysis; discipline checklists; your time management plans (one for seven tasks, one for
eight, one for nine); a reminders page of the things you personally keep forgetting; and
spare paper. Index it and flag it by colour. Do not pad it with material you have not read.

Read the pre-release one last time without your research next to you, to get the big
picture back. Then rest.

## Phase 2 — Information on the day

The information on the day may:

- give you the specific task for a topic you prepared;
- supply the inputs you were waiting for;
- **change what you expected** — you prepared to build a valuation; you are given one to
  review;
- **change what you thought you knew** — the twist. You thought the company was acquiring;
  in fact it is being acquired. You expected management's perspective; you are given the
  auditor's; or
- **take you by surprise entirely** — most often an ethics task.

Everything given on the day is there for a reason. For every line ask: why was I given
this? How does it change what I know? What am I supposed to do with it? How do I show the
marker I understood its significance?

Then do the hardest thing: **identify which of your research is now irrelevant, and
discard it.** Being ruthless here is what prevents dumping.

## Phase 3 — Planning the day

1. Read the task list first and count the tasks. Note the requested **format** of each.
2. Pull out the matching time plan. 40 minutes reading and planning, 40 minutes checking,
   the rest divided and then flexed by difficulty. Never more than 75 minutes on one task.
3. Decide the order. Consecutive is safest if you fluster easily. Strengths first builds
   confidence and banks time. Hardest first uses your freshest thinking. Whatever you
   choose, do not leave the hardest task until you are tired.
4. Take short breaks. Move. Reset between tasks.

**Quick hack:** draft the first email opening once, then copy it for the other tasks,
changing the recipient and the subject line.

## Phase 4 — Answering

See `SKILL.md` for the full answering approach. The short version:

> Audience. Components ①②③. Scope. Plan the skeleton. Lead with the answer. Say why for
> every point. Be specific. Show breadth and depth. Use the information on the day.
> Say the obvious as well as the subtle. Conclude. Check.
