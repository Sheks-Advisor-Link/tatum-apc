# Transactional writing for the APC

Transactional writing communicates facts and responses to them. Only important facts;
concise and logical; accessible to the reader. The format you are asked for signals the
level of detail expected — an email is the shortest form, a report the fullest.

**In every format: never use your real name. Sign as "Newly qualified CA(SA)" or the role
you have been given.**

## Email — the default

Most APC responses are emails, because that is how business communicates.

```
To:       [Name, title]
From:     Newly qualified CA(SA)
Date:     [date of the exam]
Subject:  [SPECIFIC — what this email is about]

Dear [Name]

[Opening: the crux, in one or two lines. If you have a recommendation, give it here.]

[Body: one idea per paragraph, topic sentence first. Headings or numbered points
where there is more than one component.]

[Close: next steps, or an offer to discuss.]

Kind regards
Newly qualified CA(SA)
```

Rules: keep it short and to the point; no textspeak; be polite; if the content is long,
put the detail in a clearly labelled **attachment** and keep the email itself as a
covering note. Attachments are a legitimate and tidy way to handle a task that needs both
a calculation and a discussion — and they let you lead with the conclusion.

## Memo — internal, interdepartmental

```
TO:       [recipients]
FROM:     [role and title]
DATE:     [date]
SUBJECT:  [very specific]
```

Introduction explains the topic and background in a decisive but polite tone. Body in
numbered or bulleted points under **informative, instructive headings** so it can be
skimmed. Short closing paragraph, positive in tone. **A memo is not signed.**

## Report — formal and comprehensive

Title page (title, author, job title, organisation, date) → abstract (~250 words, written
last: the problem, the method, the conclusions, the recommendations) → introduction
(context and scope, background, objectives and limits, method, an indication of the
recommendations) → discussion/findings (interpret and analyse; explain the significance
of each point; state the limitations you encountered and how they could be resolved) →
conclusions and recommendations (draw the points together; introduce nothing new).

## Business letter — external parties

Sender's address (omit on letterhead), date, recipient's name, title and address, then
"Dear Mrs Sithole". Topic line in capitals. First paragraph states the purpose in two
lines. Middle paragraphs: one aspect each, topic sentence first. Concluding paragraph
restates the purpose and proposes a course of action. Closing line in a full sentence
("I look forward to hearing from you."). "Yours sincerely" after a name; "Yours
faithfully" after "Dear Sir or Madam". No punctuation after the salutation.

## Minutes — past tense, formal, brief

Heading (what meeting, when, where), members present with roles in brackets, apologies,
guests, approval of previous minutes (proposer/seconder, vote, date carried), matters
arising (Item / Discussion / Action), new business (same structure; "Motion" replaces
"Discussion" where one is formally proposed), announcements, future agenda items, next
meeting details, prepared by. Record each decision and a brief rationale. Names are only
needed for a proposer and seconder. **Stay in the past tense** — "he would circulate",
not "he will circulate".

## Slides

You only have Word and Excel. Label "Slide 1", "Slide 2" and put the content beneath, or
draw a border around each. Nobody expects design; they are marking what you chose to put
on the slide. Keep points short — but not so short that you contradict yourself or lose
the argument. If the task also asks for journal entries or workings, make sure they agree
with the points on the slides.

## Working paper templates and tables

If a template is provided, **use it**, exactly as structured, with each response linked
to its reference. Tables work well when the information on the day is tabular and you are
responding to its contents — but they cost formatting time, so do not force one where
prose is faster.

## Matching tone to audience

| Recipient | What they want | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| **CEO** | Strategic consequence, concise, the decision | Formal, brief, lead with the answer |
| **CFO / Financial manager** | The detail, the standard applied, the numbers | Formal, technical, precise |
| **Board / a director** | Consequence, risk, what they must decide | Formal and respectful; flag possible contraventions, never accusations |
| **Non-executive without a finance background** | Plain explanation of the financial concept | Formal, no assumed vocabulary |
| **Audit partner** | Risk of material misstatement, high-risk items | Professional, risk-focused |
| **Audit manager** | The detail of the work and whether requirements are met | Professional, thorough |
| **A peer or junior** | Coaching, the principle explained | Collegial, questioning, constructive |
| **External party** | Only what is properly theirs to know | Formal, careful about confidentiality |

Explaining a concept to an engineer on the board is not the same as explaining it to a
fellow CA(SA). Adjust what you assume they already know.
