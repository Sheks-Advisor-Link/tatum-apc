# What markers actually penalise

Compiled from SAICA and UCT examiners' comments, 2020–2026, including the June 2026 mock
(Fishies) and the August 2026 1st QE (Diamante). These patterns repeat every single year.
Read this before every attempt.

## The seventeen recurring failures

1. **Spending too long on the first one or two tasks.** Candidates who go big early
   hardly ever finish. Not finishing is close to fatal.
2. **Not answering every part of the task.** A missed component means an incomplete
   answer, and the chance of a C is low.
3. **Ignoring the information on the day.** The most common cause of an LC.
4. **Forgetting the pre-release context.** Recommending a cash distribution for a
   cash-constrained business shows you do not understand the company.
5. **Not explaining why.** "That's wrong" is worthless. Explain what the principle is
   and why the treatment fails it. Add a conjunction — *because*, *and*, *due to* — to
   every point you make.
6. **Dumping technical content.** Writing out IFRS, King or the Companies Act earns
   nothing and signals weak communication. Highlighting one key principle is fine;
   reproducing the book never is.
7. **Thinking the goal is to apply your research.** The goal is to answer the task.
   Sometimes your research turns out to be largely irrelevant. That is fine.
8. **Fundamental errors.** Depreciation in a cash flow forecast; "IFRS says" something it
   does not; revenue only when cash is received. One of these casts doubt over everything.
9. **Writing as an outsider.** Use "I", "we", "our company". You work there.
10. **Freezing over what you don't know.** Something will catch you out. Go back to first
    principles, write down what you do know, and start.
11. **Saying or doing something unethical**, including offering to share confidential
    information. Nothing else you write will save the task.
12. **Not stating the obvious.** If the discount rate is right, say so. If a procedure is
    correct, write "Correct". If an item is properly excluded, say why. Unwritten
    knowledge is knowledge the marker cannot credit.
13. **Panicking into major rewrites.** Pause, re-reason, and change only what must change.
14. **Under-performing on financial reporting tasks.** SAICA uses FR performance as the
    key indicator of competence, and there is usually more than one FR task.
15. **Not checking your work**, so contradictions survive. Contradictions create doubt
    about competence — including between two tasks that touch the same issue.
16. **Deferring to a specialist** where you should know the answer yourself. Appropriate
    for genuinely complex valuation or tax; never for ordinary accounting and auditing.
17. **Using your real name.** Ever.

## Failures of scope and level

- **Answering wider than asked.** The single largest source of lost time and lost grades
  in the August 2026 QE. Candidates set out the whole five-step revenue model when asked
  only to review journals; built DCF and IRR models when told the analysis was an initial
  screening; proposed new audit procedures when told to critique only what was given.
- **Answering at the wrong level.** "High level" means skip the intricate mechanics —
  it does not mean skip the core principle, and it never excuses failing to apply the
  facts and dates given on the day.
- **Ignoring "you can assume", "you don't need to recalculate", "please ignore tax".**
  These are there to help you spend time well, not to trick you.
- **Unnecessary definitions.** If there is no doubt that something is a contract, do not
  define a contract.

## Failures of depth

- Identifying an issue without explaining why it matters, or what its consequence is.
- Stating a question without the reason it is relevant. Not "why has an after-tax rate
  been used?" but "why an after-tax rate, given that these are pre-tax cash flows and
  should therefore be discounted at a pre-tax rate?"
- Naming a framework without applying it. Restating a King principle as written in the
  handbook shows nothing.
- Points too brief to stand — half sentences, bare bullets, no application.
- Vague recommendations: "improve controls", "increase monitoring", "use CCTV". Specify
  who, what, where, how often, and to what end.
- Giving the same reason for several different items. If that happens, you have stopped
  thinking.

## Failures of breadth

- Raising one or two obvious issues when the case contains five.
- Repeating the same point in different words and counting it as coverage.
- Recommendations that all address one theme — six controls all about accuracy, none
  about validity or completeness; five KPIs all measuring revenue growth.
- Reviewing items in isolation when they operate as a set. KPIs work together; a set of
  controls works together; a group of reconciling items must be dealt with as a whole.
- Failing to consider more than one stakeholder group in a governance or ethics task.

## Failures of judgement

- **No conclusion.** Where a recommendation is asked for, an unconcluded analysis is an
  incomplete task — even where the numbers are close. Especially where they are close:
  then the recommendation must be carried explicitly by judgement.
- Leaving qualitative factors as a list instead of converting them into a decision the
  recipient can act on.
- Accepting management's or a tool's numbers without professional scepticism.
- Answering from the wrong perspective — the counterparty's rather than your employer's,
  the government's rather than the company's.
- Strategic points that are not contextualised to the business receive no credit.
- Recommendations that are not pragmatic for this company at this stage of its life.

## Failures of technique on specific task types

**Reviewing a calculation.** Go line by line. Say what is correct as well as what is
wrong. Consider items wrongly *included* and items wrongly *excluded* — the second is
harder and separates competent from borderline. Question the reasonableness of forecasts
and judgements, and give a comparative or reason for why you are questioning them. Then
add qualitative insight linked to the context. If asked only for errors, do not list the
correct items at length; if asked what is correct and incorrect, do both.

**Reviewing someone's work or coaching a junior.** Questioning, constructive tone.
Explain the principle, not just the verdict.

**Open-ended tasks** ("key factors", "key issues", "what should we consider").
They often need no conclusion — they test your thought process. "Key" means key: a short
list of well-explained, relevant, contextualised points beats a long list of generic
ones. Generic answers actively count against you.

**Tasks with a twist.** Expect the facts to change on the day. Use the new facts
explicitly. Even where the change is small, use some of it — it is the minimum evidence
that you are not reproducing a pre-prepared answer.

**Internal control tasks.** A weakness is a root cause plus what could go wrong, not a
restatement of the process. Every recommendation must be practical in the actual
operating environment, linked to a specific weakness, and detailed enough to implement.
Cover validity, accuracy and completeness. Separate monitoring from operations, and do
not give oversight staff operational duties.

**Audit tasks.** Procedures must be specific about what, how and on what evidence.
Distinguish management's responsibilities from the auditor's. Weigh the reliability of
evidence — internal versus external, enquiry versus corroboration.

**Tax tasks.** Identify the *specific* provision the facts engage, not the nearest
plausible one. Work through its requirements, exceptions and reliefs. Use the financial
statement extracts to identify which balances need tax revision.
