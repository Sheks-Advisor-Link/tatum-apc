# APC case study method

The APC is an eight-hour, competency-based case study. There are no marks. Two trained
markers independently grade each task and must reach consensus. You pass by being
assessed **Competent overall**, which is a profile of task results, not a percentage.

## The grading scale

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HC** Highly competent | Great answer. No major faults. |
| **C** Competent | Good answer. Useful. Would be complimented on. Could be improved. **Pass.** |
| **BC** Borderline | Not currently useful, but could become useful. **Not a pass.** |
| **LC** Limited competence | Missed the boat. **Fail.** |
| **NA** No attempt | Viewed very negatively — a competent person gets through their work. |

A Competent answer does **not** have to be perfect. It has to be *useful* to the person
who asked for it. On a seven-task paper, roughly four C's is the usual threshold, with
technical tasks (financial reporting especially) weighted most heavily as indicators.

## The four phases

1. **Pre-release (5 days).** Understand the business, identify triggers, research them
   broadly, study until you know it, build your file.
2. **Information on the day.** New facts that confirm, expand, change or reverse what you
   prepared. Everything given on the day is there for a reason.
3. **Plan.** Read the task list, count the tasks, allocate time, plan each answer.
4. **Write.** Apply the information on the day, answer every component, explain why,
   conclude.

## The answering approach — apply every time

**Before writing**

1. Identify the **audience**. CEO, CFO, board, junior colleague, audit partner, external
   party — each gets a different tone, level of detail and type of content. A board wants
   strategic consequence; a financial manager wants the standard applied; a junior needs
   coaching, not correction.
2. Number the **components** of the task: ①②③. The first is usually the most important.
   Check them off before you move on. A missing component almost guarantees a non-pass.
3. Write down the **scope**. "High level", "ignore tax", "no calculations needed", "only
   critique what is provided", "at least three" — these are instructions, not hints.
   You get no credit for anything scoped out, and you lose time you needed elsewhere.
4. Ask what this person actually needs to know. If you can answer that, you have the task.
5. Plan the skeleton: sub-headings first, then key words under each, then sentences.

**While writing**

- Lead with the answer, then support it. Recipients read the first line.
- Every point needs a *because*. If you find yourself giving the same reason repeatedly,
  you are not thinking critically enough.
- Be systematic, not scattered — review line by line, contract by contract, procedure
  by procedure. Say what is **correct** as well as what is wrong.
- Be specific. "Review the payroll report" is nothing; "the financial manager compares
  the payroll report to each project manager's authorised overtime report and signs it"
  is a control.
- Show breadth (coverage of different themes) and depth (why each matters). Six controls
  covering validity, accuracy and completeness beat six controls all covering accuracy.
- Cross discipline unless scoped out — a financial reporting answer can note the tax or
  audit consequence briefly. Don't go crazy; add a little extra value.
- Use "I", "we" and "our company". You work there.
- Never write out extracts of IFRS, the Companies Act or King. Never suggest a specialist
  for something you should know yourself.
- Quantify wherever the facts allow, and use the figures given on the day.

**Before moving on**

- Does it answer the task? Every component? Does it meet the recipient's objective?
- Have I concluded, where a conclusion is called for?
- Any contradictions with another task's answer? Both are from you.
- Any real name anywhere? Remove it.

## Time management

Eight hours. Reserve 40 minutes to read and plan and 40 minutes to check. Divide the
rest by the number of tasks, then flex: add 5–15 minutes to two or three hard tasks,
take it from the easy ones. **Never exceed 75 minutes on any task.** Never steal from
the checking buffer early in the day. Overspending on tasks (a) and (b) is the most
reliable way to fail.

## Ethics tasks

Ethics is rarely triggered in the pre-release — expect to meet it for the first time on
the day. Structure every ethical task the same way:

1. **What is the dilemma or concern?** Describe it precisely. If you cannot articulate
   it, you cannot resolve it.
2. **What bears on it?** Apply the facts plus the frameworks: SAICA CPC five fundamental
   principles and the threats to them; King V ethical and effective leadership,
   organisational ethics, corporate citizenship, stakeholder inclusivity; the Companies
   Act (s76 duties, s77 liability, s72(4) social and ethics committee); NOCLAR (CPC
   s260/s360); the ethics triangle — good for self, good for others.
3. **What should be done?** Balanced discussion of alternatives, then a clear course of
   action, in a respectful tone. "Consider" for the optional; state the mandatory plainly.

There is no such thing as a task that is "a business question, not an ethical question".
Business is an ethical endeavour. Look for the ethical dimension in every task.

## Where the technical marks are

SAICA treats performance on **financial reporting** tasks as a leading indicator of
overall competence, and there is usually more than one. Do not leave an FR task thin.
