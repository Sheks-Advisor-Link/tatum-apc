The marking standard

There are no marks. Two trained markers independently grade each task and must reach consensus if they differ. You pass by being assessed Competent overall, which is a profile of task grades — not a percentage.

GradeWhat it meansPass?
HC Highly competentGreat answer. No major faults.Yes
C CompetentGood answer. Useful. Would be complimented on. Could be improved.Yes
BC BorderlineNot currently useful — but could become useful if certain improvements were made.No
LC Limited competenceMissed the boat. The task would have to be re-performed.No
NA No attemptViewed very negatively — a competent person gets through their work.No
A Competent answer does not have to be a perfect answer. It has to be useful to the person who asked for it, and it has to deal sufficiently with the core competencies being assessed in that task. That is a much lower bar than "complete and correct" — and a much higher bar than "I wrote a lot about the right topic".

On a seven-task paper, roughly four C's is usually sufficient. Four C's alongside three LC's goes to borderline review, where the technical tasks — financial reporting above all — carry the most weight. Getting through all the tasks matters more than perfecting any one of them: a No Attempt almost always sinks the overall result.

The pre-release period — five days

What the pre-release is not. It is not an opportunity to draft answers to the tasks you think are coming. You cannot answer a single past APC task from the pre-release alone — every task depends on instructions given on the day. Candidates who arrive with pre-prepared answers and try to force them onto the actual task are the ones who fail.

It exists to give you a holistic view of the business and a list of technical areas to research. Your goal by Tuesday night is to believe you work there, and to know every triggered technical area well enough to explain it to a colleague without opening your file.

1Understand the business and industry

Read the whole pack once or twice simply to understand it — every word, every attachment, every hyperlink. Follow any reference to a real company or source; it was put there deliberately. Then answer:

  • What is unique about this company? What is its strategy, business model and income streams? What market segment does it target?
  • How is it doing financially? Run the ratios — liquidity, leverage, profitability, efficiency, working capital cycle, Du Pont. Look for significant movements and differences from industry norms, not every line item.
  • Where is it in the business life cycle — start-up, expanding, cash cow, declining, distressed? Each stage has its own risks and decisions, and your recommendations must fit the stage.
  • Who are the people? Roles, qualifications, experience, reporting lines. You will be writing to them.
  • What governance structures exist? Board composition, independence, committees. Appropriate for a company of this size and type?
  • Who are the stakeholders, and is the company dependent on any of them?
  • What decisions must be made within the timeframe of the case?

Supplement with your own research: industry reading, video, and the integrated reports of comparable JSE-listed companies. Read the chairman's report for the external environment and governance, the CEO's for operations. Summarise with a SWOT or Porter's Five Forces.

Don't over-invest here. In the exam you only get credit for points about this business, not the industry at large.

2Identify the triggers

A trigger is an event, transaction, circumstance or issue requiring professional consideration. Build a complete list, then rank it by how clear each trigger is and how many pieces of information point to it. Look for:

SignalWhat it looks like
ContentAny reference to a section of legislation, an accounting or auditing standard. Always a strong trigger.
Scoping"I'm interested in the financial reporting consequences — don't worry about tax." Narrows your research. Silence means consider every discipline.
Specific eventsAn upcoming board meeting, audit, review, decision or deadline.
Unfamiliar conceptsAnything you do not understand. Five days is plenty; there is no excuse for still being confused on the day.
UncertaintyAnything ending in a question, or where someone is concerned, confused, or weighing options.
AnalysisAnything your ratio work flags.
Industry-specific accountingThe standards that always matter for this industry, mentioned or not — IAS 41 for a farm, IFRS 16 for an airline, IAS 40 for a property group, rehabilitation provisions for a miner.

3Unpack each trigger across every discipline

This is where preparation usually fails. A trigger is an issue, not a discipline. Before researching anything, work each trigger through every lens — unless a discipline has been explicitly scoped out:

Financial reporting — initial recognition, subsequent measurement, derecognition, presentation, disclosure. Which standard applies first? Whose perspective? What's the deferred tax? What are the judgements?
Tax — not just the section. The requirements for application, the exceptions, exclusions and limitations, and the effect of applying or not applying it. Income tax, CGT, VAT, dividends tax, PAYE.
Audit — the whole cycle: planning, materiality, risk of material misstatement, responses, evidence, reporting. Plus independence and ethics.
Financial management — valuation, financing, cost of capital, cash flow, hedging.
MDMC — relevant costing, limiting factors, break-even, KPIs, budgeting, internal controls.
Strategy, risk, governance, ethics — strategic fit, risk exposure, King V, the Companies Act, stakeholders, the six capitals. Could anyone's conduct be tested here?

Draw a mind map per trigger. It keeps you honest about breadth and stops you narrowing onto the one answer you have decided is coming.

4Research broadly, then apply — and run the "what if" pass

Research the technical area, not the task you think you will get. Revise the area first, then apply it to the case facts. Keep the relevant page of the pre-release open beside your technical material so you keep seeing the trigger in context. As you go, ask continuously: which requirements appear to be met? Which are uncertain? What am I assuming? What further information would I want?

Work one trigger at a time. Note stray thoughts about other triggers on a separate page and carry on. Spend most time on the highest-ranked triggers and the content you are least comfortable with — not the subjects you enjoy.

The highest-return hour of the pre-release period: for each trigger, ask what if. What if a fact changed? What if an exception applied? What if the day adds information? Work through the alternative treatments and be able to journalise or calculate each one. The day almost always changes something. If your what-if comes up, you have already thought it through. If it doesn't, you understand the area far better anyway.

5Study, and build the file

You may take a lever-arch file (up to 8cm) and your SAICA handbooks. The file is not a substitute for knowing the material. Exam time is for engaging with new information, not for learning what you should already know. Anything in your file that you did not write yourself must still be studied.

File contents: the pre-release itself · a one-page summary per trigger plus supporting detail · SWOT and ratio analysis · articles explaining unfamiliar concepts · industry analysis · discipline checklists · time management plans for seven, eight and nine tasks · a personal reminders page of the things you keep forgetting to do · spare paper. Index it, flag it by colour, and do not pad it with material you have never read.

Read the pre-release one last time without your research next to you, to get the big picture back. Then rest properly.

Information on the day

The day may do any of five things to what you prepared:

What it doesExample
Gives you the specific taskYou knew they were considering expansion; you are given an acquisition to value.
Supplies the inputsYou expected to do a valuation; here are the numbers.
Changes what you expected to doYou prepared to build a valuation; you are given one to review.
Changes what you thought you knew — the twistYou thought the company was acquiring; in fact it is being acquired. You expected management's perspective; you are given the auditor's.
Takes you by surpriseAn ethics task you had no warning of. This is normal.
Everything given on the day is there for a reason. SAICA does not pad. For every line ask: why was I given this? How does it change what I already knew? What am I supposed to do with it? How do I show the marker I understood its significance?

Then do the hardest thing: identify which of your research is now irrelevant, and discard it. This is emotionally difficult after five days of work, and being ruthless about it is precisely what prevents dumping.

The day plan

Eight hours. Read the task list first and count the tasks — the detail of what is required is embedded in the documents, but the number tells you how to budget. Note the requested format of each response.

AllocationRule
First 40 minutesRead the information on the day and the tasks. Make planning notes. Do not start writing.
Last 40 minutesRead through and check. Do not raid this early — at 11am you have no idea how the rest of the day will go.
The middleDivide equally by the number of tasks, then flex: add 5–15 minutes to two or three difficult tasks, take it from the easy ones.
Hard ceilingNever more than 75 minutes on any task. Beyond that the marginal return is zero.

Order: consecutive is safest if you fluster easily. Strengths first builds confidence and banks time. Hardest first uses your freshest thinking. Whatever you choose, do not leave the hardest task until you are tired — and aiming to out-perform on five tasks while neglecting three is not a strategy, it is a way to fail.

Take short breaks and move. Reset deliberately between tasks so the previous one stops occupying your head.

The most reliable way to fail: going big on tasks (a) and (b), writing far too much and spending far too long. Those candidates hardly ever finish, and not finishing is close to fatal.

The answering approach

Before you write

  • Identify the audience. CEO, CFO, board, junior colleague, audit partner, external party. Each gets a different tone, level of detail and type of content. A board wants strategic consequence; a financial manager wants the standard applied; a junior needs coaching, not correction. Visualise the person — the marker is playing that role.
  • Number the components. ①②③ on the question paper. The first is usually the most important; the third and fourth matter less but must still be answered. Tick them off before you move on.
  • Write down the scope. "High level" · "ignore tax" · "no calculations needed" · "only critique what is provided" · "at least three" · "you can assume". These are instructions. You get no credit for anything scoped out, and the time costs you elsewhere.
  • Ask what this person actually needs to know. If you can answer that, you have worked out the task, whatever verb was used.
  • Plan the skeleton. Sub-headings first, then key words under each, then sentences. Keep a scrap page for points that occur to you mid-sentence.

While you write

Lead with the answer. Recommendation or conclusion in the first line or two, then the support. Busy recipients read the top.
Say why, every time. Attach a because to every point. If you catch yourself giving the same reason repeatedly, you have stopped thinking critically.
Be systematic. Line by line, contract by contract, procedure by procedure. Say what is correct as well as what is wrong.
Be specific. "Review the payroll report" is nothing. "The financial manager compares the payroll report to each project manager's authorised overtime report and signs it as evidence of review" is a control.
Breadth and depth. Breadth = the range of relevant themes covered. Depth = why each one matters. Six controls covering validity, accuracy and completeness beat six controls all covering accuracy.
Cross disciplines. Unless scoped out, briefly note the tax or audit consequence of a reporting answer. A little extra value — don't go crazy and crowd out the core.
Write as an insider. "I", "we", "our company". You work there. Never use your real name.
Use the numbers. Quantify wherever the facts allow, and use the figures given on the day, not the ones from the pre-release.
Never: write out extracts of IFRS, King, the Companies Act or an ISA · refer the recipient to a specialist for something you should know yourself · state a question without explaining why it is relevant · contradict yourself across two tasks · leave an analysis unconcluded where a recommendation was asked for.

Before you move on

  • Does it answer the task — every component of it?
  • Does it meet the recipient's objective, in the requested format and tone?
  • Have I concluded where a conclusion was called for?
  • Have I shown breadth and depth?
  • Is it acceptable work for an entry-level CA(SA)?
  • Any contradictions with another task? Both answers are from me.
  • Is my real name anywhere?

Task-type playbooks

Ethics

Rarely triggered — expect to meet it for the first time on the day. There is no such thing as "a business question, not an ethical question". Business is an ethical endeavour; look for the ethical dimension in every task. Structure every ethics task the same way:

  1. What is the dilemma or concern? Describe it precisely. If you cannot articulate it, you cannot respond to it.
  2. What bears on it? Apply the facts plus a framework. SAICA CPC: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour — and the threats to each (self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation): identify, evaluate, address. King V: ethical and effective leadership, organisational ethics, corporate citizenship, stakeholder inclusivity. Companies Act: s76 duties, s77 liability, s72(4) social and ethics committee. NOCLAR: CPC s260 / s360. The ethics triangle: good for self, good for others.
  3. What should be done? A balanced discussion of the alternatives — note the negatives of the option you favour and the positives of the others — then a clear course of action. State mandatory requirements plainly and respectfully; use "consider" for the optional.

Cover more than one stakeholder group, and link each impact to the specific facts. Generic stakeholder analysis is explicitly penalised.

Reviewing someone's work — a calculation, journals, notes, or AI output

The single most common task shape in the APC.

  • Work line by line. For each item: is it correctly included, and is the amount right?
  • Say what is correct as well as what is wrong. If you do not write it down, the marker cannot credit it.
  • Identify items wrongly excluded, not just wrongly included. This is harder and it separates competent from borderline, because it requires you to understand the principles the calculation rests on.
  • Where the work involves forecasts or judgements, question their reasonableness — and give a comparative or a reason for the question. "Why an after-tax rate?" is worth nothing; "why an after-tax rate, when these are pre-tax cash flows and should be discounted at a pre-tax rate?" is worth the grade.
  • Where the facts leave genuine uncertainty, work with the uncertainty: explain what different outcomes would follow from different facts, rather than forcing a single answer.
  • Add qualitative insight linked to the context. Context is always worth something.
  • If it is a junior's work, adopt a coaching, questioning tone. Explain the principle, not just the verdict.
  • Check consistency: if you say something should be treated a particular way, your illustrative journal must reflect it.

Open-ended tasks — "key factors", "key issues", "what should we consider"

These test your thought process and often need no conclusion. "Key" means key. Depth beats quantity: a short list of well-explained, contextualised points scores far higher than a long list of generic ones — and listing generic answers actively counts against you. Understand the purpose, plan the structure before writing, and be specific and concise.

Internal control weaknesses and recommendations

  • A weakness is the root cause plus what could go wrong as a result — not a restatement of the process.
  • A recommendation must be practical in the actual operating environment, linked to a specific weakness, and detailed enough to implement: who does it, what they do, how often, against what, and what happens on an exception.
  • Cover validity, accuracy and completeness — and separate preventative from detective controls.
  • Separate monitoring and oversight from operations. Never give an independent reviewer an operational duty that compromises their objectivity.
  • Use any stated management objective — if the board wants technology and ERP integration, a manual control is only half an answer.

Audit procedures and reporting

  • Procedures must say what evidence, how it is obtained, and why it addresses the risk. Identify the risk and therefore the direction of testing — understatement or overstatement.
  • Weigh reliability: external beats internal; original beats copy; observation and inspection beat enquiry. Enquiry alone is never sufficient.
  • Keep management's responsibilities and the auditor's distinct. Management prepares and assesses; the auditor evaluates.
  • Going concern: at least twelve months from the end of the reporting period. A material uncertainty is doubt, not a conclusion that the entity will fail. Adequately disclosed → unmodified opinion plus a Material Uncertainty section. Inadequately disclosed → qualified or adverse. Basis inappropriate → adverse.
  • Report structure: Opinion first, then Basis for Opinion. Material but not pervasive → qualified. Legal and regulatory matters go in their own section.

Relevant costing and decision-making

Only future cash flows that change as a result of the decision are relevant. Exclude sunk costs, committed costs, allocated overheads and depreciation. Include opportunity costs — the benefit forgone by using a resource you already own. Apply every adjustment consistently on both sides of the comparison, and state explicitly why each item is in or out.
  • Where a resource is scarce, compare contribution per unit of the limiting factor.
  • Answer at the level asked. A screening analysis is not an NPV; building the model you were not asked for costs you the tasks you did not finish.
  • Where the numbers come out close, say so explicitly, then carry the decision on judgement — and still reach a clear recommendation.
  • Separate what recurs from what happens once, and consider the asymmetry: what does each option cost if deferred?

Writing formats

The format you are asked for signals the level of detail expected. An email is the shortest form; a report the fullest. Never use your real name in any of them.

Email — the default

To:       [Name, title]
From:     Newly qualified CA(SA)
Date:     [date of the exam]
Subject:  [SPECIFIC — what this is about]

Dear [Name]

[The crux, in one or two lines. If you have a recommendation, give it here.]

[Body: one idea per paragraph, topic sentence first. Headings or numbered
points where the task has several components.]

[Close: next steps, or an offer to discuss.]

Kind regards
Newly qualified CA(SA)

Keep it short and to the point. No textspeak. Be polite. Where the content is long, put the detail in a clearly labelled attachment and keep the email as a covering note — this is a legitimate, tidy way to handle a task needing both a calculation and a discussion, and it lets you lead with the conclusion.

Quick hack: draft the first email opening once, then copy it for the other tasks, changing only the recipient and the subject line.

Memo — internal, interdepartmental

TO: / FROM: / DATE: / SUBJECT: (very specific). Introduction explains the topic and background, in a decisive but polite tone. Body in numbered or bulleted points under informative, instructive headings so it can be skimmed. Short, positive closing paragraph. A memo is not signed.

Report — formal and comprehensive

Title page (title, author, job title, organisation, date) → abstract, written last (~250 words: the problem, the method, the conclusions, the recommendations) → introduction (context and scope, background, objectives and limits, method, an indication of the recommendations) → discussion and findings (interpret and analyse; explain the significance of each point; state the limitations you encountered and how they could be resolved) → conclusions and recommendations (draw it together; introduce nothing new).

Business letter — external parties

Date always. Recipient's name, title and address. "Dear Mrs Sithole" (name preferred). Topic line in capitals. First paragraph states the purpose in two lines. Middle paragraphs: one aspect each, topic sentence first. Concluding paragraph restates the purpose and proposes a course of action. Closing line as a full sentence. "Yours sincerely" after a name; "Yours faithfully" after "Dear Sir or Madam". No comma after the salutation.

Minutes

Heading (what, when, where) · members present with roles in brackets · apologies · guests · approval of previous minutes (proposer/seconder, vote, date carried) · matters arising (Item / Discussion / Action) · new business (same structure; "Motion" replaces "Discussion" where one is formally proposed) · announcements · future agenda items · next meeting · prepared by. Record each decision and a brief rationale. Past tense throughout — "he would circulate", not "he will circulate".

Slides

Only Word and Excel are available. Label "Slide 1", "Slide 2" and put the content beneath, or draw a border around each. Nobody is marking design — they are marking what you chose to put on the slide. Keep points short, but not so short that the argument is lost, and make sure any journals or workings agree with the points on the slides.

A provided template

Use it, exactly as structured, linking each response to its reference. Do not restructure it, and do not add content it did not ask for.

What loses grades

Compiled from SAICA and UCT examiners' comments, 2020–2026. These repeat every year.

The seventeen recurring failures

1Spending too long on task (a) and (b). Candidates who go big early hardly ever finish.
2Not answering every part of the task. A missed component makes a C unlikely.
3Ignoring the information on the day. The most common cause of an LC.
4Forgetting the pre-release context. Recommending a cash distribution for a cash-constrained business shows you do not understand the company.
5Not explaining why. "That's wrong" is worthless. Explain the principle and why the treatment fails it.
6Dumping technical content. Reproducing IFRS, King or the Companies Act earns nothing and signals weak communication.
7Thinking the goal is to apply your research. The goal is to answer the task. Sometimes the research turns out largely irrelevant — that is fine.
8Fundamental errors. Depreciation in a cash flow forecast; "IFRS says" something it does not; revenue only when cash is received. One of these casts doubt over everything else.
9Writing as an outsider. Use "I", "we", "our company".
10Freezing over what you don't know. Expect to be caught out. Go back to first principles and start writing — starting is what unfreezes you.
11Saying or doing something unethical, including offering to share confidential information.
12Not stating the obvious. If the discount rate is right, say so. If a procedure is correct, write "Correct". Unwritten knowledge cannot be credited.
13Panicking into major rewrites. Pause, re-reason, change only what must change.
14Under-performing on financial reporting. SAICA treats FR performance as the key indicator, and there is usually more than one FR task.
15Not checking your work, so contradictions survive — including between two tasks that touch the same issue.
16Deferring to a specialist where you should know the answer yourself.
17Using your real name.

Failures of scope and level

Answering wider than asked was the largest single source of lost grades in the August 2026 QE: setting out the whole five-step revenue model when asked only to review journals; building DCF and IRR models when told the analysis was an initial screening; proposing new audit procedures when told to critique only what was given. Every one of those was explicitly ruled out by the wording on the day.

Answering at the wrong level. "High level" means skip the intricate mechanics — not the core principle, and never the application of the facts and dates given.

Unnecessary definitions. If there is no doubt it is a contract, do not define a contract.

Failures of depth

  • Identifying an issue without explaining why it matters or what follows from it.
  • Naming a framework without applying it. Restating a King principle as written shows nothing.
  • Points too brief to stand — half sentences, bare bullets, no application.
  • Vague recommendations: "improve controls", "increase monitoring", "use CCTV".
  • The same reason given for several different items — a sign you have stopped thinking.

Failures of breadth

  • Raising one or two obvious issues when the case contains five.
  • Repeating a point in different words and counting it as coverage.
  • Recommendations all addressing one theme — six controls all about accuracy; five KPIs all measuring revenue growth.
  • Reviewing items in isolation when they operate as a set. KPIs work together. A set of controls works together. All reconciling items must be dealt with.
  • Considering only one stakeholder group in a governance or ethics task.

Failures of judgement

  • No conclusion. Where a recommendation was asked for, an unconcluded analysis is an incomplete task — especially where the numbers are close, because then the recommendation must be carried explicitly by judgement.
  • Leaving qualitative factors as a list instead of converting them into a decision the recipient can act on.
  • Accepting management's — or a tool's — numbers without professional scepticism.
  • Answering from the wrong perspective: the counterparty's rather than your employer's.
  • Strategic points not contextualised to the business. These receive no credit.
  • Recommendations that are not pragmatic for this company at this stage of its life.

Final check — before you upload

  • Every task attempted. No blanks.
  • Every component of every task answered.
  • Each response in the format requested, addressed to the right person, in the right tone.
  • Information on the day applied in every task — specific facts, specific figures.
  • Every point carries a because.
  • A clear conclusion or recommendation wherever one was asked for.
  • Nothing included that was scoped out.
  • No contradictions between tasks.
  • No extracts of standards or legislation reproduced.
  • My real name is nowhere in the document.
  • Spellcheck run.
"Opportunity comes to the prepared mind." There is no way to know what the case will cover. With thorough, relevant preparation and a plan you stick to, you can handle whatever it is.
