How to use this document

Each task below sets out, first, the approach — how the task should have been read and planned on the day — and then the answer in the form and tone the task asked for. The approach boxes exist because the APC is not marked on knowledge alone: it is marked on whether you answered what was asked, applied the information on the day, and communicated in a way that is useful to the named recipient.

The three things that decide every task. (1) Did you answer every component of the task, in the requested format, to the named audience? (2) Did you apply the information on the day rather than reproduce pre-release research? (3) Did you explain why — breadth (what) plus depth (why it matters)?

Answers are written in the first person as the newly qualified CA(SA) at Diamante. Nowhere is a real candidate name used — in the APC you are never yourself, you are the role.

Contract 1 — 1 000-carat parcel sold on 1 June 2026

What I agree with

ItemWhy it is correct
Recognising revenue on 1 June 2026This is the date the tender was awarded, which is the point at which control of the parcel passes to the buyer under our accounting policy. The timing of recognition is not in question here.
Recognising the receivable at the spot rate of R16.30 (R26 080 000)Correct. IAS 21 requires a foreign currency transaction to be recorded on initial recognition at the spot rate ruling at the date of the transaction. W1 applies the right rate to the right quantity.
Recognising the FEC on the statement of financial position at 30 JuneCorrect in principle. A forward exchange contract is a derivative and must be recognised and measured at fair value, so an entry at reporting date is required. The error is in the direction and the destination of the gain, not in the fact that an entry is needed.
Using the 30 June spot rate as a proxy for the forward rate in measuring the FECStrictly, the fair value of an FEC is the difference between the contracted forward rate and the forward rate available at reporting date for the remaining term, discounted. With one day to maturity the forward rate is effectively the spot rate and discounting is immaterial, so the approach is acceptable. I would document that judgement in the file.

Errors

ErrorExplanation and correction
Revenue recognised at the June average rate of R16.38 (W2) Revenue should be recognised at the spot rate on the transaction date, R16.30, giving R26 080 000 — the same amount as the receivable. An average rate is only permitted as a practical approximation where there is a large volume of transactions and rates do not fluctuate significantly. Neither applies: this is a single, dated, material transaction with a known spot rate, and exchange rate volatility is one of our five key risks. Revenue is currently overstated by R128 000. It is also internally inconsistent to translate the two legs of one transaction at two different rates.
Foreign exchange loss of R128 000 recognised on 1 June (W3) This is not an exchange difference — it is the balancing figure created by the error above. No exchange difference can arise on initial recognition, because both the debit and the credit are translated at the same rate on the same date. Exchange differences arise only on subsequent settlement or retranslation. Correcting entry: Dr Revenue R128 000 / Cr Foreign exchange loss (P/L) R128 000.
The FEC treated as a liability and a loss The FEC is a financial asset and gives rise to a gain. We have contracted to sell US$1.6 million at R16.55 when the market rate at 30 June is R16.42. We will therefore receive 13 cents more per dollar than the market would give us — the contract is favourable to us, so it is an asset. The entry as drafted reverses both the classification and the sign, and overstates the loss for the year at a time when our results are already under pressure.
The fair value movement taken to other comprehensive income It must go to profit or loss. A stand-alone derivative is measured at fair value through profit or loss under IFRS 9. Routing it through OCI is only available where the instrument has been formally designated and documented as a hedging instrument in a qualifying hedge relationship at inception, which we have not done. Nor would cash flow hedge accounting be available even if we wanted it: the hedged item here is a recognised monetary asset, not a highly probable forecast transaction. At most this could have been designated as a fair value hedge — and the fair value movements would still have gone to profit or loss.
The amount of R272 000 does not agree to W4 W4 states 1 000 × 1 600 × (16.55 − 16.42), which is R208 000, not R272 000. R272 000 is US$1.6 million × (16.55 − 16.38) — the average rate appears to have been used a second time. The narration also describes the FEC as a liability, which compounds the classification error above. Please ask that this be re-performed and re-cast as a gain.

OmittedExplanation

The entry required is Dr Receivable / Cr Foreign exchange gain (P/L) of R192 000 (US$1.6m × R0.12). Contract 2 — the "Eternal Flame" 40-carat blue diamond

Conclusion first: no revenue should be recognised in FY2026. The US$700 000 deposit received on 20 June should be recognised as a contract liability at 30 June 2026, and the full US$2.7 million recognised as revenue in FY2027 on 15 July 2026. My reasons follow.

The performance obligation is satisfied at a point in time

There is a single performance obligation — to transfer the diamond. None of the over-time criteria are met: the Purchaser does not simultaneously receive and consume any benefit; we are not creating or enhancing an asset the Purchaser controls; and although the stone is highly specific, we do not have an enforceable right to payment for performance to date — the Purchaser could cancel and recover the deposit if verification failed. Revenue is therefore recognised at the point in time at which control transfers, and the question becomes when that is.

Applying the indicators of transfer of control at 30 June 2026

IndicatorMet?Application to the facts on the day
Present right to paymentNoWe held the US$700 000 deposit, but the US$2 000 000 balance was not due until 15 July. We did not have a present right to the consideration for the stone at the reporting date.
Legal titleNoThe contract expressly provides that legal title passes only once the final payment clears, which happened on 15 July.
Physical possessionNo, in substanceThe stone was moved on 28 June to a neutral third-party vault in Switzerland, not to the Purchaser's premises. The Purchaser holds the only biometric keys to the viewing case, but is contractually prohibited from moving or selling the stone. Holding a key while being barred from using, moving or disposing of the asset is not possession in substance — the Purchaser can look at the diamond but cannot do anything with it. I have also considered whether this is a bill-and-hold arrangement: it is not, because the reason for the custody arrangement is our own protection pending payment and verification rather than a substantive request by the customer, and the stone was not ready for physical transfer while verification was outstanding.
Significant risks and rewardsNo, on balanceThe contract passed the risk of accidental damage or theft to the Purchaser on 28 June, which points towards transfer. But two facts on the day cut the other way. First, we maintained our own global insurance policy on the stone until 15 July — we would not insure an asset we regarded as economically someone else's, and the Head of Finance's own note ("just in case") shows we considered ourselves exposed. Second, the risk transferred is narrow. Casualty risk in a high-security vault is remote; the risks that actually matter commercially — that the stone fails verification, and the price risk until then — remained with us.
Customer acceptanceNoThis is the decisive indicator. The sale was subject to a Final Scientific Verification, which only took place on 5 July, and the Purchaser could cancel and recover the deposit if the stone was not internally flawless. Because even minor flaws significantly affect value, this is a substantive acceptance clause, not an administrative formality, and we could not objectively determine at 30 June that the agreed specifications had been met — determining that was the whole purpose of the verification. That the stone passed on 5 July does not change the FY2026 position: we assess control on the facts available at the reporting date.

Taken together, four of the five indicators point clearly against transfer, and the fifth is heavily qualified by our own insurance. Control passed on 15 July 2026, when the balance cleared, title passed and the stone was released to the Purchaser's vault — three things happening together. I want to be explicit that the conclusion is not "revenue follows payment": payment happens to coincide with the transfer of control in this contract, which will not always be the case.

Consequences for the FY2026 financial statements

  • Contract liability. The US$700 000 deposit is recognised as a current contract liability, not as revenue and not as a deposit received against a receivable. I note the contract describes it as non-refundable while also providing that the Purchaser could recover it if verification failed — it is therefore not truly non-refundable, which is a further indicator that the customer had not accepted the asset. We should not rely on the "non-refundable" label.
  • Translation of the deposit. The contract liability is a non-monetary item, so it is not retranslated at 30 June — it stays at the spot rate on 20 June when the cash was received. Under IFRIC 22, that same 20 June rate is also the rate at which the portion of FY2027 revenue funded by the deposit will be measured; only the remaining US$2 million is translated at the 15 July spot rate. This is easily missed and gives a different revenue figure to translating the whole US$2.7 million at one rate.
  • Inventory. The diamond remains in our inventory at 30 June. Its net realisable value is comfortably supported by the US$2.7 million contract price, so no write-down arises — a useful point given the pressure on inventory values elsewhere. The transport cost we paid is a fulfilment cost of the contract.
  • Variable consideration. At 30 June the consideration was arguably variable and constrained, since the verification outcome could have ended the contract. The uncertainty resolved on 5 July, so there is no measurement consequence — but it reinforces why recognition at 30 June would have been wrong.
  • Event after the reporting period. The conclusion of the sale on 15 July is a non-adjusting event after the reporting period: the conditions arose after year-end. US$2.7 million is approximately R44 million at the 30 June rate, which is about 2.4% of FY2026 revenue of R1 863 million, and the transaction is qualitatively material given the rarity of the stone and our current financial position. I recommend we disclose its nature and estimated financial effect.
  • Going concern. The US$2.7 million was received in July and is real cash in the refinancing window. It should be reflected in the cash flow forecasts supporting the going concern assessment, and made available to KZL.

Happy to talk any of this through, and to draft the correcting journals and the after-reporting-date note if that is useful.

Attachment — Feedback on the proposed accounting treatments

Transaction 1 — Sale of Mine 2

Your proposal: the only impact is the derecognition of the related assets and liabilities when the mine is sold in August 2027.
My view: not appropriate. The accounting consequences begin when the mine is classified as held for sale, which on your timeline is likely to be around October 2026, not on completion in August 2027.

Nothing changes in FY2026 — but check the dates

At 30 June 2026 no decision had been taken; the board only considers the matter in October 2026. The FY2026 financial statements are therefore unaffected. However, if the board resolves to sell before those financial statements are authorised for issue, the resolution is a non-adjusting event after the reporting period and we would need to disclose its nature and estimated financial effect. Given the refinancing timetable, that is a real possibility and I would keep it on the reporting checklist.

From board approval — IFRS 5 classification

IFRS 5 applies once the carrying amount will be recovered principally through a sale transaction rather than through continuing use. Two criteria must be met:

  • Available for immediate sale in its present condition. This appears to be met. Mine 2 is already a separate cash-generating unit for impairment purposes, with its own PPE, receivables, inventory, payables, provisions and cash, and there is nothing in the proposal suggesting further work is needed before it could be sold as it stands.
  • The sale must be highly probable. This requires management at an appropriate level of authority to be committed to a plan, an active programme to locate a buyer to have been initiated, active marketing at a price reasonable in relation to fair value, and completion expected within one year. On the facts you have given, all four are likely to fall into place in October 2026: the board resolution provides the commitment and authority, the search for a buyer follows immediately, the intention to market at around the recoverable amount supports a reasonable price, and the expected 10-month process is within twelve months. The fact that completion takes 10 months does not prevent classification — the period is based on our own and industry experience and is within the limit.

What classification actually does — and why the proposal understates the impact

  • Because the assets and liabilities will be disposed of together in a single transaction, Mine 2 is a disposal group. Its assets and its liabilities are presented separately on the face of the statement of financial position, and are not offset against each other.
  • The disposal group is remeasured to the lower of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell, with any write-down recognised in profit or loss. I would flag that the recoverable amount used for the FY2026 impairment was most likely value in use; fair value less costs to sell is a different measure and we should not assume the two are equal. A further loss beyond the R333 million already recognised for Mine 2 is possible.
  • Depreciation ceases on the mine's depreciable assets from classification. That alone changes FY2027 profit materially — group depreciation of mining assets was R675 million in FY2026.
  • Prior-period statement of financial position comparatives are not restated.

Discontinued operation

We must also assess whether Mine 2 is a discontinued operation, and I expect it will be. It is one of only two mines we own, it generated R630 million of FY2026 revenue — about a third of the group — and it is separately reported in our revenue note. It is therefore a separate major line of business. The consequence is a separate single-line presentation of the post-tax result of the discontinued operation in profit or loss, with comparatives re-presented, and separate disclosure of its cash flows. This is the same presentation we applied to the Botswana mine, so the board has seen the effect before.

On completion

In August 2027 we derecognise the disposal group and recognise the difference between the net proceeds and the carrying amount in profit or loss — but by then most of the loss will already have been taken through the IFRS 5 remeasurement, so the completion entry should not be large. One caution from the Botswana sale: if the consideration is deferred or contingent on the mine's future cash flows, it must be recognised at fair value, which was assessed at nil in that case. If the buyer proposes similar terms here, the accounting outcome could be very different to the headline price, and the board should understand that before agreeing the structure.

Transaction 2 — KimberTrust contributes R400m for a 30% share

Your proposal: recognise a R400 million gain in profit or loss on 1 November 2026, and thereafter continue to recognise 100% of Mine 2's revenue and expenses.
My view: not appropriate on both counts.

This is a joint arrangement, and specifically a joint operation

The arrangement gives Diamante and KT joint control: they are bound by a contractual arrangement, and KT "participates with Diamante to collectively direct all aspects relating to the operations of Mine 2" — that is, decisions about the relevant activities require the unanimous consent of both parties. Neither party controls Mine 2 unilaterally, and together the two hold all of it (70% / 30%).

A joint arrangement is then either a joint operation or a joint venture, depending on whether the parties have rights to the assets and obligations for the liabilities, or rights to the net assets. Here the agreement expressly gives KT a share of "30% of the assets and liabilities of Mine 2" and of the revenue and expenses thereafter. It is therefore a joint operation, and each party accounts for its share of the assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses. Equity accounting would only be correct for a joint venture.

Two cautions on classification. If the final agreement is instead structured through a separate incorporated vehicle, the legal form, the contractual terms and other facts and circumstances all have to be reassessed before concluding — a separate vehicle points towards a joint venture unless the terms reverse that. And KT is not a non-controlling interest: NCI only arises where we control a subsidiary, which is not the case if control is genuinely joint.

The R400 million gain

We should not recognise a R400 million gain. In substance we are disposing of a 30% interest in the assets and liabilities of Mine 2 for R400 million. On 1 November 2026 we derecognise 30% of the carrying amount of Mine 2's assets and liabilities and recognise the difference between the R400 million and the net carrying amount given up as a gain or loss in profit or loss. The gain is R400 million less 30% of Mine 2's net assets — which, given the R333 million impairment, could be a meaningful gain, but not the full R400 million. Recognising the whole amount would overstate profit by the carrying amount of what we have given up.

Thereafter we recognise only 70% of Mine 2's revenue and expenses, and 70% of its assets and liabilities. Recognising 100% would overstate both revenue and costs and would misrepresent our interest in the mine to users who are focused on our production base.

What I would want confirmed before finalising

The description says KT contributes R400 million "towards funding the operations of Mine 2". Whether that cash is paid to Diamante or into the mine changes the analysis — in the second case part of the contribution funds jointly held assets rather than buying out our interest, and the gain would be smaller or absent. I would also want to know whether any part of the R400 million relates to future services or obligations we must still perform. Please could we see the draft agreement when it is available.

Transaction 3 — Closure of Mine 2

Your proposal: recognise a liability for all of Mine 2's employee costs relating to the closure when the mine closes in October 2027.
My view: not appropriate. The employee population has to be split, and for both groups the proposed timing is wrong.

Employees who are redeployed

These employees continue to render service to Diamante in their new roles, and their remuneration is not a termination benefit. Under IAS 19 the general principle applies: their costs are recognised as short-term employee benefits as they render the service. No liability is raised now, and certainly not in October 2027, for salaries they will earn in future periods — those are future operating costs, not a present obligation.

Employees who are retrenched

These amounts are termination benefits, and the obligating event is the termination of employment, not the service rendered. IAS 19 therefore requires recognition at the earlier of:

  • the date on which we can no longer withdraw the offer of those benefits; and
  • the date on which we recognise the costs of a restructuring within the scope of IAS 37 that involves the payment of termination benefits.

A restructuring provision arises under IAS 37 only once there is a detailed formal plan — identifying the operation concerned, the principal locations, the approximate number of employees to be compensated, the expenditure and the timing — and a valid expectation has been raised in those affected, either by starting to implement the plan or by announcing its main features to them. A board decision alone is expressly not enough. On your timeline, the trigger is most likely the point at which we announce the closure to, and conclude the negotiations with, the unions and employees. That is likely to fall in FY2027, well before October 2027. Deferring recognition to the closure date would understate our liabilities and overstate profit in the intervening period. Where settlement is expected wholly more than twelve months after the reporting period, the benefits are discounted and treated as other long-term benefits.

Other consequences of closure — flagged, not analysed

You asked about employee costs specifically, so I have dealt with those first. For completeness, the closure decision would also require us to consider:

  • Rehabilitation and decommissioning. Closing earlier changes both the amount and the timing of the obligation. Our provisions currently stand at R558 million non-current and R63 million current, and a revision under IAS 37 and IFRIC 1 could be significant.
  • Asset lives and impairment. Mining assets depreciated over a units-of-production basis will need their remaining reserve base and useful lives revised, and closure is itself an impairment indicator.
  • Discontinued operations. An asset to be abandoned is not classified as held for sale, but Mine 2 would still be presented as a discontinued operation from the date it ceases to be used.
  • Onerous contracts. Any supply or offtake commitments we can no longer fulfil from Mine 2 would need assessment.
Two points for the board, beyond the accounting. First, whichever route is chosen, the decision and its timing feed directly into the going concern assessment and into what we tell KZL and our lenders — the bank commitment is conditional and our lenders will read a Mine 2 decision as information about the sustainability of the business. Second, the three options have very different cash profiles: a sale brings in consideration that may be deferred, the KT partnership brings in R400 million of third-party cash against a R333 million cash balance, and closure costs cash in retrenchment and accelerated rehabilitation. That difference may matter more to the refinancing than the accounting does.

Attachment — Assessment of the proposed campaign

1. What exactly the concern is

The proposed statement asks consumers to accept that Diamante's natural diamonds are "simply the most ethical and sustainable choice". That is a comparative superlative. It claims superiority not only over lab-grown diamonds but, read plainly, over every alternative — including every other natural diamond producer. Exco has recorded three things: that the statement cannot be objectively substantiated in all circumstances; that comparing the sustainability of natural and lab-grown diamonds is not straightforward because both have advantages and disadvantages; and that the prevailing view was to proceed anyway because the commercial benefit outweighs the governance and reputational risk.

That last point is the heart of it. A claim that turns out to be wrong is a mistake. A claim we publish knowing we cannot support it is a decision — and it is judged as one.

2. Why the comparison cannot in fact be substantiated

This is worth stating properly, because it is what makes the claim indefensible rather than merely bold:

  • Natural diamond mining creates real value — employment, skills development, local procurement, community investment, royalties and taxes in the regions where we operate. Our traceability under the Kimberley Process, our never mixing production between the two mines, our rehabilitation plans and our greenhouse gas roadmap are genuine and, as far as I can see, better than many peers.
  • Mining also has unavoidable impacts — land disturbance, biodiversity loss, water consumption in a water-constrained country, tailings risk and Scope 1 and 2 emissions we are still working to reduce by 35–40% by 2030. We disclose these ourselves, in our own reporting.
  • Lab-grown diamonds avoid land disturbance but are energy-intensive, so their footprint depends almost entirely on the energy source used. Some are produced on renewable power; others are not. There is no single answer.

Different stakeholders legitimately weight these factors differently — a mining community and a climate-focused consumer will reach different conclusions from the same facts. That is not a gap in our evidence that better research could close. It is the nature of the question, and it is precisely why an absolute claim cannot be made.

3. The ethical implications

The dilemma is real: we have a legitimate commercial need to defend the natural diamond category while competing with lab-grown producers and while we are refinancing, and an equally legitimate obligation not to mislead the people we are selling to. What makes this resolvable rather than genuinely balanced is that there is a course of action which serves both — a narrower claim that is true — so we are not actually forced to choose between commercial success and honesty.

  • The claim is misleading by design, not by accident. Its persuasive force comes from consumers believing it is an objective finding. If it were presented as our opinion, it would not work — which tells us that the effectiveness of the campaign depends on the very impression we know is unsupported.
  • The argument that consumers cannot evaluate complexity, so we should tell them the conclusion, is the point that concerns me most. It inverts the purpose of corporate communication. Our role is to give stakeholders reliable information so that they can decide; it is not to decide on their behalf and then supply the conclusion. It is also the sentence most likely to be quoted back at us if this is ever challenged publicly or in a regulatory process.
  • Judged against the "good for self, good for others" test, the campaign is good for Diamante in the short term and is not good for the consumer, who pays a premium on the strength of a claim we cannot prove. That asymmetry is what marks it as an ethical problem rather than a marketing judgement.
  • My own position as a CA(SA). Under the SAICA Code of Professional Conduct I cannot knowingly be associated with information that is materially false or misleading, or that omits or obscures information in a way that makes it misleading. That obligation applies to me as a professional accountant in business, not only to financial information. ProcedureExplanation
A1Correct. Inspecting the settlement agreement for its terms and for the signatures of authorised representatives of both parties is an appropriate procedure to establish whether the conversion has been formally agreed and is legally enforceable, which is the starting point for assessing whether it can be taken into account in the going concern assessment at all.
A2Incorrect, for two reasons. First, the memorandum of incorporation records the number of shares the Company is authorised to issue, not the number in issue; the number in issue is evidenced by the securities register maintained under section 50 of the Companies Act. Second, the comparison is stated the wrong way round: the point of the procedure should be to establish that the shares to be issued, together with those already in issue, do not exceed the authorised share capital — and if they do, that the MOI has been amended accordingly. As drafted the procedure would obtain the wrong document and draw the wrong conclusion from it.
A3Incorrect as drafted. The issue of shares is authorised by the board of directors under sections 38 and 40 of the Companies Act, so the evidence sought should be the board minutes and the board resolution, not the minutes of a general meeting and a special resolution. The procedure identifies the wrong authorising body and would not obtain evidence of the board's authorisation at all. For completeness — this does not make the procedure correct, but it should inform the replacement — shareholder approval may additionally be required: by special resolution under section 41(3) if the shares to be issued carry voting power exceeding 30% of the voting power of the shares in issue immediately before the issue, and under the JSE Listings Requirements because Shareholder A, holding 36%, is a related party. Both should be considered, but neither displaces the board authorisation the procedure omits.
A4Incorrect. Performing the liquidity, solvency and cash flow analysis is management's responsibility, not ours. Management is required to assess the Company's ability to continue as a going concern, and our responsibility under ISA 570 is to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about, and to conclude on, the appropriateness of management's use of the going concern basis — which means evaluating management's assessment, the basis on which it was prepared and the reasonableness of its assumptions. Performing our own assessment in place of management's would assume a management responsibility and create a self-review threat to our independence.
A5Incorrect. The conversion takes place after 30 June 2026 and, on the information available, has not yet been agreed. It is a non-adjusting event after the reporting period, so the loan balance at year-end is unaffected by it and would correctly not be nil. A procedure designed to confirm a nil balance would lead us to challenge a correctly stated balance, and would miss the work that is actually required, which relates to disclosure and to the going concern assessment.
A6Incorrect, for two reasons. First, as with A5, the share issue occurs after the reporting date, so the share capital balance in the statement of financial position and in the statement of changes in equity at 30 June 2026 is not affected by it. Second, share certificates evidence individual holdings and cannot support the total share capital balance; the securities register maintained under section 50 of the Companies Act is the appropriate record for that purpose.
B1Correct. Inspecting the amended loan agreement for its terms and for the signatures of authorised representatives is appropriate and is well directed here, because it is the precise wording of the deferral condition that determines whether the deferral is within the Company's control — which in turn affects both the going concern conclusion and whether the loan is properly classified as current or non-current.
B2Incorrect. Board minutes, approved budgets and cash flow forecasts are all internally generated by the party whose assumptions are being challenged, so they cannot corroborate those assumptions. Under ISA 500, the reliability of audit evidence is greater when it is obtained from independent sources outside the entity. Whether and when the market value of the Company's liabilities will exceed the market value of its assets is a forward-looking market-based question, and evaluating management's assumptions about it requires external evidence — diamond price and market forecasts, and, given the valuation judgement involved, consideration of whether an auditor's expert is needed under ISA 620.
B3Incorrect. Enquiry alone does not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence, and this enquiry is directed at the very party whose commitment is being tested, so it is neither independent nor free of incentive — Shareholder B has every reason to describe its own capacity favourably. A response of this kind must be corroborated with objective evidence of Shareholder B's financial position, together with direct written confirmation of the commitment and the legally binding amended agreement.
B4Incorrect, in three respects.
• The assumption is not supportable. The deferral only operates for as long as the market value of liabilities exceeds that of assets. Assuming that no repayments will be made for a full twelve months substitutes a fixed assumption for the estimate that management is required to make about how long that condition will persist, and that estimate is what we should be evaluating.
• It assumes a management responsibility. As with A4, assessing cash flow, liquidity and solvency is management's role; ours is to evaluate the assessment management has performed.
• The period is measured from the wrong date. In terms of IAS 1, and consistently with ISA 570, the going concern assessment covers a period of at least twelve months from the end of the reporting period, not from the date of approval of the financial statements.

Working Paper Review Template (completed) — Working Paper F4

ErrorExplanation
1A material uncertainty has been equated with a conclusion that the Company cannot continue as a going concern. The paragraph states that the events and conditions indicate a material uncertainty and then that "we believe the company cannot continue as a going concern". Those are different conclusions, and the second does not follow from the first. A material uncertainty exists when events or conditions have been identified that may cast significant doubt on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern — doubt, not a determination that it will fail. Our preliminary assessment is that management has failed to disclose that uncertainty, not that the Company is unable to continue. As drafted the paragraph also contradicts itself, because a belief that the Company cannot continue would call for a different opinion entirely.
2The reason for the qualification is incompletely described. Management's note acknowledges the financial challenges but neither states that a material uncertainty exists nor discloses the plans it has in place to deal with it. ISA 570 requires disclosure of both the principal events or conditions and management's plans to address them. The paragraph should identify specifically what has been omitted, so that a reader can understand the nature of the misstatement rather than being left to infer it.
3The paragraph asserts that the financial statements do not comply with the going concern basis of accounting. That is a different — and far more serious — finding than the one we have made, and it is inconsistent with a qualified opinion. We have not concluded that management's use of the going concern basis is inappropriate; had we done so, ISA 570 would require an adverse opinion regardless of whether the matter was disclosed. What we have concluded is that a required disclosure is inadequate, which is a material misstatement that is not pervasive, and that is what supports a qualification. The paragraph must distinguish between the two.
4The reference to a contravention of sections 29 and 30 of the Companies Act does not belong in this paragraph. The Basis for Qualified Opinion section explains the matter giving rise to the modification of our opinion on the financial statements. A statutory contravention, if we conclude one has occurred, is dealt with in the "Report on Other Legal and Regulatory Requirements" section, and would separately need to be evaluated as a possible reportable irregularity under the Auditing Profession Act. Including it here confuses the basis for the qualification with a separate reporting responsibility.
5The opinion itself has been included in the basis paragraph. Under ISA 700 and ISA 705 the "Qualified Opinion" section comes first, under its own heading, and is followed by the "Basis for Qualified Opinion" section. As drafted the report has no Opinion section at all: the opinion sentence ("except for the effects of the matter described above…") has been absorbed into the basis paragraph, and the two appear in the wrong order.
6The entity and the applicable financial reporting framework are described inaccurately. The paragraph refers to "Diamante (Pty) Ltd"; Diamante is a JSE-listed public company, and the same error appears in the working paper header. The framework should be described consistently as IFRS Accounting Standards as issued by the International Accounting Standards Board, together with the requirements of the Companies Act of South Africa. Precision matters in the report itself, where the wording is prescribed.
7The effects of the misstatement have not been described. Where a qualification arises from an inadequate disclosure, ISA 705 requires us to describe the omitted information in the Basis for Qualified Opinion section and, unless prohibited by law or regulation, to include the omitted disclosures where it is practicable to do so and we have obtained the information. If it is not practicable, we should say so and explain why. The draft leaves the reader with no indication of what the disclosure should have contained.
Not an error, but worth noting: the final sentences dealing with our independence, our ethical responsibilities and the sufficiency and appropriateness of the audit evidence are correctly stated and correctly placed, and should be retained.
One matter for the file that the template does not ask for. We should record that we considered whether a "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section is required and concluded that it is not: that section is used where the disclosure of the uncertainty is adequate. Where it is not adequate, the matter is dealt with through a modified opinion, as here. Documenting that we turned our mind to it will save the question being asked on review.

The transferable lessons

Scope discipline. Three of the eight tasks were lost mainly by answering something wider than what was asked — the five-step model in (a), a full DCF in (d), new procedures in (h). Every one of those was explicitly ruled out by the wording on the day. Read the scope sentence twice and write it at the top of your plan.
Always say why. "The rate is wrong" earns nothing. "The rate is wrong because IAS 21 requires the spot rate at transaction date, so revenue is overstated by R128 000" earns the grade. Attach a because to every point.
Say the obvious. A1 and B1 are correct. Block A's grade data is reliable. The deposit is a contract liability. If you do not write it down, the marker cannot assume you knew it.
Close the loop. An analysis without a recommendation, or a list of factors without a conclusion, is an incomplete task. Where the numbers are close, say so, and then say what you would do anyway — and why.
